# Judge Biases and Pipeline Controls: Position, Length, and Self-Preference

## Motivation

Once you have a rubric (chapter 01), the naïve pipeline is: build the prompt, call the judge, take the score. That pipeline is measurably wrong. Zheng et al. 2023 ("Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", <https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685>) established three systematic biases in LLM-as-judge that survive prompt engineering and appear across judge model families:

- **Position bias** — the judge prefers the answer in one slot (usually the first) even when the answers are swapped.
- **Verbosity / length bias** — the judge prefers longer answers, independent of content quality.
- **Self-enhancement / self-preference bias** — the judge prefers outputs generated by itself, or by models in the same family.

Later work (Panickssery et al. 2024, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations", <https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076>; Dubois et al. 2024, "Length-Controlled AlpacaEval", <https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475>) sharpened the picture and added length-controlled and self-preference-controlled comparison methods. All of them share one property: **prompt hardening alone does not remove the bias.** You have to build controls into the pipeline itself.

This chapter walks the three biases, the standard mitigations, how to wire the mitigations without doubling your judge cost, and how to diagnose whether the mitigations are working. If you have not applied at least the position-bias and length-bias controls in this chapter, do not gate a release on a judge score.

## Core concepts

### Position bias and swap-and-average

Position bias is the tendency of a pairwise judge to prefer the candidate presented in one slot (typically first) independent of content. Zheng et al. document that it appears on the majority of judge models tested and that it can flip a nontrivial share of judgements when candidates are near-tied on quality. It is the single most-cited bias in LLM-as-judge and the one with the cleanest mitigation.

**The mitigation: swap-and-average.** For every pairwise comparison, run the judge **twice** with the candidates in swapped positions, then aggregate.

The aggregation rule is:

- **Both runs prefer the same candidate (A-then-A → wins A; B-then-A → still wins A).** Report that winner.
- **The two runs disagree (A wins in one order, B wins in the other).** Report `tie`. The disagreement is the position-bias signal; forcing a winner in this case is what the mitigation is designed to prevent.
- **Either run returned a tie.** Report `tie` (or the winner of the non-tie run, per your policy — pick one and stick with it).

Two consequences to plan for:

1. **Cost.** Swap-and-average doubles judge calls per pairwise decision. Budget for this in chapter 05's tier routing.
2. **Tie rate goes up.** By design. If your tie rate under swap-and-average is well above what your rubric expects, the underlying decisions are near-tied — that is honest information, not a bug in the mitigation.

Implementation sketch (framework-agnostic):

```python
def pairwise_judge(candidate_a, candidate_b, criterion_prompt, judge):
    ab = judge(render(criterion_prompt, first=candidate_a, second=candidate_b))
    ba = judge(render(criterion_prompt, first=candidate_b, second=candidate_a))
    # `ab.winner` is in {"first", "second", "tie"}; normalise to A/B/tie.
    ab_winner = {"first": "A", "second": "B", "tie": "tie"}[ab.winner]
    ba_winner = {"first": "B", "second": "A", "tie": "tie"}[ba.winner]
    if ab_winner == ba_winner and ab_winner in ("A", "B"):
        return {"winner": ab_winner, "position_bias_flag": False,
                "rationales": [ab.rationale, ba.rationale]}
    return {"winner": "tie", "position_bias_flag": ab_winner != ba_winner,
            "rationales": [ab.rationale, ba.rationale]}
```

Attach the two rationales and the `position_bias_flag` to the judgement so mod-109 human reviewers can audit near-tie decisions and so chapter-05 drift monitoring can track the flag rate over time.

**Do NOT** attempt to "fix" position bias by only running one order and instructing the judge in prose to ignore position. Zheng et al. showed the bias survives the instruction; you get the cost saving without the correctness.

### Length / verbosity bias

Verbosity bias is the tendency of a judge to prefer longer answers, even when the extra length adds no substantive content. It matters more than position bias for A/B tests between candidates whose length distributions differ — e.g., a new prompt template that produces longer replies will look better than it is.

There are two mainstream mitigations, and you often use both:

**Length-normalised scoring.** After collecting judge scores, model score as a linear function of length, and report the length-adjusted residual. The AlpacaEval length-controlled paper (Dubois et al. 2024) fits a logistic regression of win probability against the length difference between the two candidates and reports a "length-controlled" win rate that de-weights length. The framing is: *what would this score look like if both candidates had the same length?* For product surfaces where reply length is not itself an SLI, length-controlled scores are the number to gate releases on.

**Length constraint in the rubric.** For absolute rubrics, include a level that penalises verbosity: "a level 5 answer is complete without being padded." For pairwise, add to the tie policy: "call a tie if the only meaningful difference is length." This partially removes the bias at the source; it does not remove all of it (the model still weights longer inputs), which is why you also do post-hoc length control.

**Do NOT** attempt to normalise by truncating candidates to a fixed length before judging. Truncation loses signal, and it will hide real quality differences that live in the last paragraph.

Length control is only meaningful when you record candidate lengths on the judgement (`app.judge_candidate_a_len`, `app.judge_candidate_b_len` for pairwise; `app.judge_candidate_len` for absolute). Add these attributes when you build the pipeline — retrofitting is painful.

### Self-preference / self-enhancement bias

Self-preference is the tendency of a judge model to prefer outputs generated by itself, or by models in its own family. Panickssery et al. 2024 demonstrate that this holds across several frontier judge families and that judges can partially recognise their own generations. In a product setting, this shows up as:

- You judge `gpt-4o` vs `claude-3.7-sonnet` using `gpt-4o` as judge; the frontier score favours `gpt-4o` by a margin that shrinks or flips when you re-judge with `claude-3.7-sonnet`.
- You upgrade your generator to a new model family; the online-eval judge (still the old family) reports a regression; the regression disappears when you re-judge with the new-family model.

Unlike position and length bias, self-preference has **no cheap unbiased mitigation**. It is a design constraint. The mainstream tactics:

1. **Cross-judge diagnostic.** Periodically (at least once per major generator or judge upgrade) re-run a sample of judgements with a different judge model family. If the two families disagree on a nontrivial share of decisions, self-preference is contaminating the primary judgement. Report the cross-judge agreement rate as a first-class metric on your judge dashboard.
2. **Ensemble judges.** Run two judges from different families and require agreement (or majority of three) to promote a decision. Expensive and only worth doing on release-gate decisions, not per-request online SLI.
3. **Prefer human calibration for the model family the generator lives in.** Chapter 04 quick-calibration: if the generator is `claude`, include human-scored replies from `claude` in the gold set, so cross-family drift shows up as a calibration miss.
4. **Do not use the same model for judge and generator on the same criterion.** Especially not for competitive A/B where the "win" attaches to the same model as the judge.

The diagnostic to run at least once per quarter: pick a stratified sample of ~100 recorded judgements, re-judge them with a second-family judge, and compute the agreement rate. If it drops below your rubric's rated inter-rater agreement threshold (chapter 04), the primary judge model is contaminated and needs replacement or ensembling.

### Other biases worth naming

Position, length, and self-preference are the three biases you build controls for. Several others appear in the literature and are worth naming so you can spot them in the wild:

- **Bandwagon / familiarity bias.** The judge prefers answers that match a "typical" pattern for the domain. Mitigation: diversify the calibration set (chapter 04).
- **Format bias.** The judge prefers a specific output format (bulleted, markdown, JSON) that it was trained to reward, independent of content. Mitigation: normalise formats before judging, or include format-mismatched examples in the calibration set.
- **Refusal bias.** The judge treats a refusal as either "safe = high score" or "unhelpful = low score" without consulting the surface's policy. Mitigation: the rubric's `not_applicable` value from chapter 01, and a separate safety rubric (mod-108) rather than folding refusals into a helpfulness score.
- **Sycophancy / affirmation bias.** The judge is nudged by the surrounding prompt language toward one answer. Mitigation: strip user-facing framing from judge prompts; keep them neutral.

None of these have a canonical single mitigation. The point is to name them in your judge design doc so the on-call knows what to look for.

### Building the controls into the pipeline (not the prompt)

The controls above are pipeline-level concerns — a loop, a second call, a post-hoc regression — not prompt-level. Wrap them at the judge-runtime boundary so every rubric benefits:

```
Judge runtime
├─ absolute(...)   → one call; attach length; return score
├─ pairwise(...)   → two calls with swap; return winner + flag
├─ ensemble(...)   → N calls with distinct judge families; return majority
└─ length_adjust(scores, lengths, ...) → post-hoc regression per rubric
```

Each entry point emits a span (an `EVALUATOR`-kind span from mod-102's schema) with `app.judge_model`, `app.judge_prompt_hash`, `app.judge_swap_order`, `app.judge_position_bias_flag`, and the candidate lengths. The trace can then answer "why did this judgement come out this way" without needing to re-invoke the judge.

### Diagnostics: is your control working

A control is worth what its telemetry says it is worth. For each mitigation, publish a metric — ideally as a small dashboard your on-call opens after every judge upgrade.

- **Position-bias flag rate.** Share of pairwise judgements where swap-and-average returned `tie` because the two orders disagreed. A very low rate (< 1%) is suspicious — either your candidates are far apart on quality, or the judge is not reasoning about the criteria enough for position to matter; either can be worth investigating. A very high rate (say > 25%) is also suspicious — either candidates are near-tied, the rubric is under-specified, or the judge is unreliable.
- **Length correlation of raw scores.** For absolute rubrics, correlation between raw score and candidate length. If it is materially non-zero, either length is a real quality signal on your surface (defensible) or length bias is contaminating your scores. Length-controlled scoring is how you separate the two.
- **Cross-family agreement.** Share of judgements where a second-family judge agrees with the primary. A drop below your calibration threshold (chapter 04) is a stop-ship signal.

Attach these three metrics to the judge dashboard mod-107's online-eval loop reads. Chapter 05 wires them into the drift monitor.

### What the pipeline does *not* fix

Bias controls are a floor, not a ceiling. They mitigate systematic biases; they do not compensate for:

- A bad rubric (chapter 01).
- A judge model whose training distribution is far from your surface (chapter 03 covers the framework and OSS-judge choice).
- A calibration set that is too small or unrepresentative (chapter 04).
- A judge tier that is too weak for the decision at hand (chapter 05).

Do all five. The controls in this chapter are the cheapest to run — do them by default and put the telemetry on the dashboard on day one.

## Summary

- Three systematic biases in LLM-as-judge that are documented in the literature and survive prompt-hardening: **position bias**, **length / verbosity bias**, and **self-preference / self-enhancement bias**.
- **Position bias mitigation is swap-and-average** — every pairwise judgement runs twice with candidates swapped, disagreement collapses to `tie`. Doubles cost; raises tie rate; the raised tie rate is honest information.
- **Length bias mitigation is dual**: constrain length in the rubric, and length-adjust scores post-hoc using a regression on candidate length. Record `app.judge_candidate_len` on every judgement so you can length-adjust later.
- **Self-preference is not fully removable** — mitigate via cross-family diagnostics on a stratified sample, ensemble judges for release-gate, and human calibration in the generator's family.
- Wrap all controls at the judge-runtime boundary — one code path per rubric shape, one EVALUATOR span per call, `app.judge_*` attributes on the span so telemetry is trivial to compute.
- Publish three diagnostics: position-bias flag rate, length correlation of raw scores, cross-family agreement rate. Read them after every judge or generator upgrade.
